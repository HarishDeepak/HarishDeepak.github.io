Testing a driver monitor the way a car maker would

New cars in Europe must watch the driver. Euro NCAP's rule is simple: eyes closed for 3 s means "asleep", and the warning has to come within 0.2 s of that moment. I built a monitor from open tools and spent most of the time on the part that usually gets skipped: checking that rule honestly.

Stack
MediaPipe, DINOv2, CLIP, CARLA, ROS 2
Data
6 sources, real + synthetic
Output
scored suite + PDF report
Code
driver-attention-bench

The pipeline

MediaPipe face points give eyelid opening, head pose and gaze. Timers turn per-frame states into Euro NCAP SD-202 warnings: microsleep (1.5 s), sleep (3 s), distraction (3 s). A harness then scores every warning against labelled truth (±0.2 s, Wilson confidence intervals, false alerts per hour) and writes a PDF report. The same warnings drive a car in CARLA and are published as ROS 2 topics.

The biggest jump on real drivers came from the test, not the detector: scoring only where labels exist, a longer calibration, and counting "closed" only when the lids are actually shut, which is what SD-202 times.

What didn't work

Even after the fixes, 27 of 49 real events are still missed or late, at 19 false alerts per hour. This is far from a product.

The drowsiness level fired on 0 of 6 alert videos, but caught only 2 of 6 self-rated drowsy ones. It detects repeated microsleeps, not early, quiet drowsiness.

Small datasets throughout: 6 DMD drivers, 2 hand-labelled night videos, simplified car physics.

What a warning is worth on the road

In CARLA, a driver who stays asleep gets warning → slow-down → a minimal-risk manoeuvre: hazards, move right if the lane is free, stop at the edge. Plugging the measured accuracy into a Monte Carlo at 50 km/h, the crash rate for an inattentive driver falls from 87% with no camera to 44% with the measured eye-closure detector, against 17% for a perfect one.
